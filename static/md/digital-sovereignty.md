# Digital Sovereignty

> Sovereignty starts with infrastructure: open, auditable, portable — and secure.

Source: https://the-future.world/digital-sovereignty/

## Digital Infrastructure

Digital systems are infrastructure today, like roads and electricity: when they are opaque, insecure or non-cancellable, democracy loses control.

## Our Principle

Local or regional preference only for critical infrastructure and sensitive data. Otherwise: open competition — but always with auditability, portability and security as requirements. (The Swiss chapter, for example, applies a Swiss/EU-first rule to critical systems.)

## The Sovereignty Package

### 1. Anti-Lock-in Law

- Exit plans as a contractual requirement
- Open standards and format interfaces
- Data portability and interoperability

### 2. Open-by-default in Government

- Open source where sensible (and maintainable)
- Funding for maintenance (not just "build and forget")
- Transparency reports on large government IT systems (risks, dependencies, exit status)

### 3. Security of Critical Systems

- Minimum standard: controls, audits, incident processes
- Supply-chain security: SBOM, signed builds, traceable provenance
- Clear responsibilities instead of jurisdictional fog

## Why This Improves Everyday Life

The same logic applies to artificial intelligence: see [Sovereign AI & Compute](https://the-future.world/sovereign-ai/).

- Fewer IT investment failures
- Less dependency on individual vendors
- Lower risk in critical services
- More trust through transparency

## Frequently asked questions

### What does digital sovereignty mean in practice?

Digital sovereignty means that governments and citizens retain control over their data, systems and digital infrastructure — independent of individual vendors or countries.

### Does that mean we can no longer use foreign software?

No. A local or regional preference applies only to critical infrastructure and sensitive data. For everything else: open competition — but always with mandatory auditability, portability and open standards.

### What is an anti-lock-in law?

A law that prevents governments from permanently tying themselves to a single IT vendor. It requires exit plans, open formats and data portability as contractual obligations.

### Isn't open source more expensive than commercial solutions?

Not necessarily. Open source reduces long-term costs through less licence dependency. What matters is that maintenance is also funded — not just the initial deployment.

### How does this affect everyday life?

Fewer IT failures in public services, fewer data breaches, more transparency. And no surprises when a vendor suddenly doubles prices or discontinues their service.
